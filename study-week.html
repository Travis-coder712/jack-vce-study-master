The baseline

The standard week

This is the default. It runs unless Jack deliberately changes it on Saturday — and it's what everyone can assume is happening on any given afternoon.

Where the time goes

Six subjects, weighted deliberately

Maths gets the most because it's the subject that punishes gaps — miss a week and the next topic doesn't make sense. Business Management and VET get the least because they're the most self-contained.

Worth knowing

Thursday and Friday nights carry 2½ of the 8 hours — nearly a third of the week sits in the two slots most likely to be missed.

That's by design, not by accident: they're the two that can be banked to the weekend. But it does mean a fortnight of skipped Thursdays and Fridays quietly costs a third of the plan, so they're the first thing to look at if the week keeps coming up short.

The reasoning

Why it's shaped like this

30

Half an hour is the unit

Short enough to start without dread, long enough to finish something. Two or three of them stacked feels like a normal afternoon, not a study session.

M

Maths every single day

Methods is cumulative and it's the one that unravels fastest. Four self-study blocks plus the Monday tutor means Jack touches it five days out of seven.

T

Tutoring is the skeleton

Monday 5:00 and Wednesday 5:40 are fixed points in the week. Everything else was placed around them, not the other way around.

B

Tuesday runs straight through

Maths, VET and Business Management back-to-back from 4:00 to 5:30 — no tutoring to work around. Bus Man sits last because it follows the Tuesday double period, so it builds on a lesson that's still fresh.

PM

Thursday and Friday move late

Those two nights sit at 8:00pm, after whatever else is on. They're the most likely to be missed — which is fine, because they're the two that can be banked to the weekend.

—

The weekend stays empty

Nothing is scheduled Saturday or Sunday by default. That's deliberate: it's the buffer that absorbs a missed night or a SAC, and it only gets used when it's needed.

Flexibility, with edges

Changing the week without losing it

The plan is meant to flex — that's the point of setting it weekly. These are the rules that keep flexing from quietly turning into not doing it.

Never moves
The two tutoring sessions, and Maths appears every weekday. If a Maths block has to go, it moves — it doesn't disappear.
Trade 1-for-1
Everything else is swappable inside the same week. A SAC in one subject buys blocks from another, and the total stays at 12.
Bankable
Thursday and Friday evenings can be carried to the weekend. Monday–Wednesday can't — they're too close to the tutoring to skip.
The floor
A subject can drop to zero for one week to make room for a SAC — never two weeks running. Cap any single subject at 3 blocks.

Worked example · Business Management SAC on Friday

Drop Economics and VET to one block each for the week, and give both back to Business Management. Bus Man goes 1 → 3; the total stays at 12; next Saturday it resets to baseline.

The weekly habit

Saturday afternoon: set the week

Twenty minutes on a Saturday is what makes the other eight hours work. The point isn't the timetable — it's knowing what's due, and deciding what each block is actually for.

Open the planner and reset to baseline 1 min

Start from the standard week every time. Don't carry last week's changes forward.

Look up what's actually due 7 min

SACs, assignments, prac reports, tests, presentations — for the next two weeks, not just this one. This is the step that gets skipped and it's the one that matters.

Check what's on 2 min

Sport, work, birthdays, family things. Mark the blocks that realistically won't happen and move them rather than pretending.

Reprioritise using the trade rules 3 min

Shift blocks toward whatever's due first. Keep the total at 12.

Write one job per block 5 min

"Eco: 3 exam questions on market failure" — not "do some Economics". A block with a named job starts in ten seconds; a block without one starts in ten minutes.

Copy it into the phone calendar 2 min

Every block as its own event with a 5-minute alert. If it isn't in the calendar it isn't a plan, it's an intention.

Sunday: a two-minute look 2 min

Anything cropped up since yesterday? Add a weekend block now if it has, rather than discovering it on Wednesday night.

Making it count

How to spend thirty minutes

Thirty minutes of real work beats two distracted hours, and it's the difference between a plan that works and a plan that just looks good on paper.

01

Know the job before you sit down

It was written on Saturday. Open the book to the right page and go. Deciding what to do at the desk eats the first ten minutes every time.

02

Phone in another room

Face-down on the desk still costs you — the brain keeps a little attention on it. Out of the room, on silent. Nothing in thirty minutes can't wait.

03

Nothing else on the screen

No YouTube "for background", no Discord, no music with words. If the block needs a laptop, close every other tab before starting.

04

Produce something, don't just read

Re-reading notes feels productive and barely works. Close the book and write what you remember, do questions, explain it out loud. If nothing's on the page at the end, the block didn't happen.

05

Hardest thing first

The first ten minutes are the sharpest ten minutes. Don't spend them colour-coding a summary.

06

Stuck for five minutes? Write it down and move on

Note the question, take it to the tutor or the teacher next lesson. One problem should never eat a whole block.

07

End with one line about where you got to

"Up to Q14, chain rule still shaky." Next block picks up instantly instead of re-reading to find the spot.

08

Actually take the break

Thirty on, then stand up and leave the desk. Two blocks run back-to-back without a break just becomes one ordinary hour.

For Mum and Dad

Our part in it

The plan belongs to Jack — he sets it, so he owns it. Our job is to protect the time and ask about the plan, not to audit the blocks.

Worth doing

  • Protect the slots. No jobs, errands or dinner debates at 4:00 Monday or 8:00 Thursday. If the time is real to us, it stays real to him.
  • One check-in a week — Saturday, when the plan gets set. "What's on this week?" That's the whole conversation.
  • Ask about the plan, not the marks. "What are you working on Tuesday?" lands very differently from "have you done your homework?"
  • Notice the streak, not the block. Four weeks of a set plan is the win, whether or not every block happened.
  • Keep the weekend genuinely free when the week went well. The buffer only works if it's a reward for using it.

Best avoided

  • Checking mid-block. Walking in at 4:15 to see if it's happening ends the block.
  • Treating a missed block as a problem. Missing one is normal and the plan is built for it. Missing three weeks running is the actual signal.
  • Rewriting his plan for him. If the weighting looks wrong, ask why it's set that way — a plan he was handed is a plan he'll drop.
  • Adding subjects or hours when things get tense. Eight hours done properly beats twelve resented ones.
  • Letting Saturday quietly slide. This is the one habit worth being firm about — everything else follows from it.
